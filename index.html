From wave optics to ray optics
A single-slit experiment at fixed wavelength. Make the slit wider and watch diffraction shrink toward the straight-through ray-optics limit.
Wave optics visible
Light passes through a single slit of width a and lands on a screen at distance L. The simulation panel is that screen: the bright vertical strip at the left is the screen itself, and the curve shows intensity as a function of screen position. The slit opening in the schematic changes live with the slit-width slider.

The pale horizontal band marks one screen-resolution bin Δx. When the diffraction width becomes much smaller than Δx, the detailed wave pattern is no longer resolved and the straight-through ray picture becomes useful.

What the plot shows

The plot is the single-slit diffraction pattern on the screen. The vertical direction is screen position x, and the curve extending to the right shows the intensity at that position.

I(x) ∝ sinc²(πax / λL)

The first minimum is at approximately |x₁| ≈ Lλ / a. So making the slit wider makes the diffraction pattern narrower.

Wavelength λ650 nm
Width ratio a / λ76.9

The diffraction pattern is wide compared with the screen resolution, so wave optics is essential.
Why the pattern shrinks

For a single slit, the first minimum occurs at approximately |x₁| ≈ Lλ/a. At fixed wavelength, increasing the slit width a makes the whole diffraction pattern narrower.

Why this becomes “ray optics”

When the slit width is much larger than the wavelength, the diffraction width becomes tiny compared with the screen resolution Δx or the scale of interest on the screen. The wave is still there, but the straight-through beam is then a very good approximation.
