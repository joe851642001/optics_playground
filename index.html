Diffraction pattern

Light passes through a single slit of width \(a\) and lands on a screen at distance \(L\); the right-hand panel is that screen. The intensity as a function of screen position \(x\) is

\[ I(x) \propto \operatorname{sinc}^2\!\left(\frac{\pi a x}{\lambda L}\right) \]

with the first minimum at \(|x_1| \approx L\lambda/a\), so a wider slit gives a narrower pattern.

Why this becomes ray optics

When the slit is much wider than the wavelength, the diffraction width \(2L\lambda/a\) becomes tiny compared with the screen resolution \(\Delta x\). The wave is still there, but the straight-through beam is then a very good approximation.

What to look for

Finding the image with three rays

From the tip of the object, draw any two of the three principal rays; the image tip is where they cross after the lens. The third ray is a check.

  1. A ray parallel to the axis leaves the lens through the far focal point F′.
  2. A ray through the centre of the lens continues in a straight line.
  3. A ray through the near focal point F leaves the lens parallel to the axis.

If the rays diverge after the lens, extend them backwards (dashed): they meet at a virtual image on the object side. For a diverging lens, rays 1 and 3 are aimed at the focal points rather than through them.

Reflection from the curved surface

The ray intersection is found on the spherical mirror itself. At each hit point the radius toward C is the local normal, and the outgoing direction obeys \( heta_i= heta_r\). The tree marked “Paraxial image” is the Gaussian prediction from the mirror equation. Away from the axis, exact spherical rays can miss that point slightly: that is spherical aberration, not a drawing error.

Ideal thin-lens model: the object lies at \(F_1\) of the first lens, while the retina lies at \(F'_{\rm eye}\), 17 mm behind the eye lens.

The eye is 85 mm behind the first lens. The incoming rays are still parallel, so the ideal eye focuses them exactly on the retina.

What to look for

The rays leave the first lens parallel to each other. Sliding the eye changes where they hit the eye lens, not their angle, so the eye always focuses them to the same point on its back focal plane: the retina. A real finite aperture would eventually cause vignetting if the eye moved outside the beam.

What to look for

When the object is at the correct distance, the intermediate image lands on the focal plane of the eyepiece. The eyepiece then sends out nearly parallel rays, and the fixed human-eye lens focuses them onto the retina.

Move the object away from its correct distance and the retina is no longer sharply focused, even though the objective, eyepiece and eye lens stay fixed.

What to look for

Keplerian: the objective forms a real, inverted intermediate image; placing it at the eyepiece’s front focal plane makes the outgoing rays parallel. Galilean: the diverging eyepiece sits before that image would form, with its (virtual) focal point on the objective’s focal point, so the tube is shorter and the view upright, but there is no real image to put a reticle on.

Move the eyepiece away from normal adjustment and the outgoing rays converge or diverge, so the fixed eye lens no longer focuses sharply on the retina.
